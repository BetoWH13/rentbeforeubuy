What warranty or protection actually comes with a former rental car?

There is no single “former-rental warranty.” The protection on a used former rental depends on the exact vehicle, who is selling it now, what written warranty remains, and what other policies apply to this transaction.

Factory warrantyAny remaining manufacturer coverage tied to the specific VIN and its time/mileage limits.
Seller warrantyAny written warranty the current seller includes with this sale.

Start with the exact VIN and the current seller

A former rental may be sold by the rental company itself, by a franchise or independent dealer, or by a later owner. The car's prior use does not tell you which protections apply today.

Ask the current seller for the written warranty and return/buyback terms that apply to the exact VIN. If the seller says manufacturer coverage remains, verify that coverage using the VIN rather than assuming the remaining time or mileage.

If you are buying from a dealer, read the Buyer’s Guide

The FTC says covered used-car dealers must display a Buyer’s Guide telling you whether the car is being sold with a warranty or “as is,” and what portion of repair costs the dealer will pay under any dealer warranty. The FTC also advises buyers to get promises in writing.

Do not rely on a salesperson saying “you’re covered.” Read the actual Buyer’s Guide, warranty document, and sales contract together. If they conflict, stop and get the discrepancy resolved in writing before buying.

A return policy is not a warranty

A return or buyback period answers a different question from repair coverage. It may give you a short window to unwind the transaction under stated conditions, but it does not necessarily pay to fix defects after that window closes.

The FTC notes that federal law does not generally give buyers a three-day right to cancel a dealer vehicle purchase. Return rights can depend on state law or on a seller's own policy, so get the exact terms in writing rather than assuming a cooling-off period exists.

An “extended warranty” may actually be a service contract

The FTC distinguishes a warranty from an auto service contract. A service contract is generally a separate paid product with its own covered repairs, exclusions, term, deductible, repair network, and cancellation rules.

Before buying one: compare the service contract against any remaining manufacturer warranty and seller warranty. Paying twice for overlapping protection is not automatically useful.

What to get in writing before you sign

  1. The exact VIN covered.
  2. Who is responsible for the warranty or policy.
  3. What systems and components are covered.
  4. What is excluded.
  5. Time and mileage limits.
  6. Deductible or buyer share of repair costs.
  7. Where repairs must be performed.
  8. Whether towing, rental-car reimbursement, or other incidental costs are included.
  9. Return/buyback conditions, if any.
  10. Any separate service-contract price and cancellation terms.
